More than a billion people live with a mental-health condition. Even in higher-income countries only about half of them get care; in low-income countries, fewer than one in ten.3 Gaming itself is rarely the illness — an estimated 0.3–1.0% of the general population in the US may meet the criteria for gaming disorder.4 The point is reach: many of the people who need support already spend their evenings in games.

The solution is simple: real servers, hosted by real specialists.

Create communities inside the places people already play — hosted by psychologists, psychiatrists and trained specialists. People keep playing, keep being online, but now inside a space where a specialist is close by. It's a small shift that changes everything.

Why

Hours don't predict harm. Why people play does.

The World Health Organization added gaming disorder to ICD-11 in 2019, and it has applied since 2022;5 the American Psychiatric Association's DSM-5-TR still lists it only as a condition for further research.6 Meanwhile a University of Oxford study that logged real play from nearly 40,000 players found no meaningful link between hours played and wellbeing. What mattered was why people played: those who felt they had to play reported worse wellbeing; those who simply wanted to play did not.7 Motivation research has described the same split for years — play that meets needs for enjoyment, competence and connection, versus play driven by pressure.8 Choice versus compulsion — that difference is the whole idea behind this platform.

Why → What

We're building a support platform, not a clinic.

We believe in a better future for young people online — and that even digital spaces can genuinely help, not just harm. reh.ab is prevention and early support: the step before treatment, and the bridge to it.

Why a specialist

Often the game isn't the problem. It's where the problem shows up.

Research on “compensatory” internet use suggests that heavy play is often a way of coping with something else — stress, low mood, loneliness.9 A moderator can stop a fight. A specialist can notice the person behind it, and help them find the right kind of support.

When play does become a problem, the best-studied help is cognitive behavioural therapy.10 Games can even be part of treatment: SPARX, a CBT game for teenagers with depression, worked as well as usual care,11 and in 2020 EndeavorRx became the first game the US FDA authorised as a treatment, for ADHD.12 Specialists who play also know the risks particular to games — such as loot boxes, whose spending is linked to problem gambling.13

What

To build this space we need the best — psychologists, psychiatrists, specialists who understand games.

How

We go where gamers already are.

No new app to download, no new habit to build. Guardian servers run where players already gather — Discord, TeamSpeak, and in-game chat and voice wherever a game lets a community host its own space.

Where we find our specialists

Worldwide there are about 13 mental-health workers per 100,000 people.3 A gap that size doesn't close through one channel — so we don't rely on one.

  1. 01

    Discover

    We post through specialist job boards and physician communities — not general hiring sites.

  2. 02

    Vet

    We check every licence, run a background check, and interview each person twice: once on their clinical record, once on whether they really know games.

  3. 03

    Onboard

    Matched to a guardian server by specialty and by the games they already play — not assigned at random.

Why

For many young people, the friends they play with are real friends. It's time we made those spaces safe too.

One in five adolescents and young adults is lonely, according to the World Health Organization.14 And three in four adult players in the US said they were harassed in online games in 2023.15

How

Support that steps up only when it's needed.

  1. 01

    Play

    Peers and trained moderators keep the room friendly. Most evenings, that's all anyone needs.

  2. 02

    Notice

    The specialist on the server notices when someone is struggling, and offers a private word — never forced.

  3. 03

    Check in

    A short conversation and an optional self-check questionnaire. No diagnosis, no therapy on the server.

  4. 04

    Refer

    If someone needs more, we help them reach real care — a doctor, a therapist, a local service — with someone else, not the person they play with.

  5. 05

    Emergency

    In danger right now: your local emergency number. In most EU countries, 116 123 is the emotional-support line and 116 111 the line for children and young people.

How

AI stays behind the scenes.

It's a support tool for our specialists — task lists, time tracking, technical utility. Nothing more. It never replaces a human professional.

How

AI is part of this field whether we like it or not.

Clinicians use it today for the work around care — notes, summaries, admin — so more of their time goes to people. We're building on how the field works, not against it.

In the American Psychological Association's 2025 survey, 56% of US psychologists had used AI tools in their work in the past year — mostly for writing and paperwork, rarely for clinical decisions.20 The same association warns that chatbots and wellness apps are no substitute for care.21 That is the line we hold: AI helps the specialist and never replaces them.

Proof

How we'd know it works.

A twelve-week pilot on three guardian servers, measured against comparable servers without a specialist. Four numbers decide whether it continues.

  1. 01

    Wellbeing

    The WHO-5 wellbeing index — five questions, opt-in, at the start and the end.22

  2. 02

    Reaching care

    Of the people referred on, how many actually reach a doctor, therapist or service.

  3. 03

    Safety

    Harassment reports and safety incidents, compared with servers that have no specialist.

  4. 04

    Cost

    Specialist hours per active player — the number that decides whether it can scale.

If wellbeing doesn't move, or safety gets worse, the model changes — or the pilot stops.

We aim to meet WCAG 2.2 level AA. The page has been checked for keyboard access, screen-reader labelling, reduced motion and target size, and the accessibility controls in the top-left corner let you turn motion off, raise contrast and change text size at any time.

One known contrast gap. White text on the bright red sections measures 4.24:1 — enough for the large headlines, short of the 4.5:1 AA needs for smaller text. Turn on High contrast and the whole page switches to yellow on black (18.6:1), with framed panels and buttons, which passes at every size.
